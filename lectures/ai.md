# AI Engineering: A Short Interview Lecture

AI engineering is the work of turning a language model into a useful, reliable system. For an interview, it helps to think in four layers: how the model works, how it gets the right information, how it takes actions, and how you measure and operate the result.

## The model and its context

Start with the language model itself. Most modern large language models are decoder-only transformers that generate text one token at a time. A tokenizer splits text into pieces, and the transformer uses self-attention to relate each piece to earlier pieces. The model predicts a distribution over the next token, selects one, adds it to the sequence, and repeats. It is producing plausible continuations, not consulting a built-in database of verified facts.

Generation has two distinct phases. During prefill, the model processes the whole prompt in parallel; this phase is limited by compute. During decoding, it generates output tokens one at a time; this phase is limited by memory bandwidth. A key-value cache saves earlier attention computations so they are not repeated, but each new token still costs a full pass. That is why output tokens are priced higher than input tokens and why long answers are noticeably slower. Attention cost also grows quadratically with context length, so a huge prompt has a real performance price.

Tokens matter beyond billing. Tokenization explains several classic quirks: models are poor at counting letters, weak at arithmetic on long numbers, and spend more tokens on non-English text. The context window is the total token budget for input and output in one call. Even if a model accepts a very long context, quality degrades as the context fills, a problem often called context rot, and details buried in the middle are easy to miss, which is known as "lost in the middle." Put important instructions at the start or end, retrieve relevant information when needed, and compact old conversation turns into summaries rather than carrying everything forward forever.

How does a model learn this behavior? First comes pretraining on next-token prediction over trillions of tokens, which produces a base model with broad knowledge but no instruction-following. Then supervised fine-tuning teaches it to follow examples of instructions and answers. Preference tuning pushes it toward answers people prefer. In reinforcement learning from human feedback, a reward model learns from human rankings, and the model is then optimized against it, classically with PPO. Direct preference optimization, or DPO, skips the separate reward model and learns directly from preference pairs. RLAIF and Constitutional AI replace human labels with AI feedback. Reasoning models add large-scale reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards on math and code, using methods such as GRPO, popularized by DeepSeek-R1. They learn to think at length before answering. That is test-time compute: spending more tokens at inference time buys accuracy, at the cost of latency and money.

At inference time, sampling controls how the next token is chosen. Temperature scales how sharply the model favors likely tokens. Low temperature suits extraction, classification, and code; higher temperature adds variety for creative work. Top-k restricts the choice to a fixed number of likely tokens. Top-p, or nucleus sampling, chooses from the smallest set whose probabilities add up to a threshold. Tune temperature or top-p, not both. Even a temperature of zero does not guarantee identical runs, because batching and floating-point arithmetic on GPUs can change results.

Good instructions are the first engineering tool. Prompt engineering means stating the task, audience, constraints, and output format clearly, often with a few examples, which is called few-shot prompting. Separate sections with delimiters or XML tags, ask for step-by-step reasoning on hard problems, and explain why a rule exists, not just what it is. Context engineering is broader: it decides which instructions, documents, conversation turns, memories, tool descriptions, and tool results the model sees at each step. Too little context forces guesses; too much hides the useful material and increases cost. For agents, context engineering is the main lever for quality: retrieve just in time through tools, trim large tool outputs, and hand isolated subtasks to subagents with clean contexts.

## Knowledge, evidence, and evaluation

When the model needs facts from your documents, retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is often the right choice. First, split documents into useful chunks, attach metadata, turn the chunks into embeddings, and index them. At question time, retrieve relevant chunks, rerank them with a cross-encoder for precision, and give them to the model with instructions to answer only from them and cite the evidence. This keeps answers current without training a new model.

An embedding is a vector representing the meaning of text. Nearby vectors, usually measured by cosine similarity, tend to represent related passages. Use the same embedding model for indexing and querying; changing the model means re-embedding everything. Exact search is linear in the collection size, so vector stores use approximate nearest-neighbor indexes. HNSW, the most common default, uses a layered proximity graph; IVF searches only the nearest clusters; product quantization compresses vectors to save memory. Faster search can lose some relevant results, especially when metadata filters are involved. A useful retrieval system often uses hybrid search: it combines vector similarity with BM25 keyword search and merges the two rankings with reciprocal rank fusion, because exact names and identifiers are easier to find with words than with meaning.

If a RAG answer is poor, inspect retrieval before rewriting the generation prompt. A bad document split, a missing metadata filter, or a mismatch between the question and the document wording may mean the right evidence never reached the model. Query rewriting helps with that mismatch, and so does HyDE, which embeds a hypothetical answer instead of the question. Measure retrieval recall separately from whether the final answer is faithful to the retrieved text. In agentic RAG, search becomes a tool the model can call repeatedly, so it can refine a query or gather more evidence before answering.

Hallucination is a fluent claim that is false or unsupported. It happens because a model predicts likely text, and training rewards answering over admitting uncertainty, so a confident tone is not proof. Ground factual answers in sources, allow the model to say it does not know, ask it to quote the source before answering, and lower the temperature for factual tasks. Verify important claims with searches, code, or database queries. Citations help only when they actually support the claim. You cannot eliminate hallucination, so design the product and its review process around it.

To choose between prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning, ask what is missing. If the task is already within the model's capabilities, improve the prompt first. If it lacks knowledge that is private, recent, or extensive, use RAG. If it lacks a behavior, such as a consistent format, style, or narrow task, consider fine-tuning. Fine-tuning is also how you distill a large model into a smaller, cheaper one. Low-rank adaptation, or LoRA, trains small adapter matrices while leaving the base weights frozen; QLoRA does the same on a 4-bit quantized base model, so large models fit on a single GPU. Fine-tuning needs good examples and an evaluation showing that it beats a well-prompted baseline. It is an awkward way to keep a changing fact base up to date.

Evaluation tells you whether any of these choices helped. Build a set of realistic questions and known failure cases, then run it whenever you change the model, prompt, or pipeline, ideally as a regression test in CI. This is eval-driven development, the counterpart of test-driven development. Prefer deterministic graders, such as schema checks and unit tests, where they fit. A model can grade subjective qualities against a rubric, but an LLM judge has biases: it favors certain positions, longer answers, and its own outputs. Calibrate it against human review. For RAG, check retrieval and faithfulness. For agents, check whether the task actually succeeded, not whether every step matched one expected script, and check consistency: pass@k asks whether any of k attempts succeeds, while pass^k asks whether all of them do. In production, also watch user feedback, cost, latency, and traces of failures.

If an application needs machine-readable output, specify a JSON schema and enforce it with constrained decoding, which masks invalid tokens during generation, or with a strict tool interface. Validate the result anyway: a valid JSON object can still contain the wrong date, amount, or decision. Strict formats can slightly hurt reasoning, so let the model reason first and emit the structured result last. Without built-in enforcement, validate the output in code with a library such as Pydantic or Zod, and retry with a clear error when necessary.

## From model calls to agents

An agent is a model using tools in a loop. The model sees a task, chooses a tool call, receives the result, and decides what to do next. This reason-then-act loop is known as ReAct. The surrounding program, often called the harness, executes tools, maintains context, and enforces step limits, budgets, and permissions. The model does not execute a tool merely by naming it. Errors compound across steps: at 95 percent reliability per step, a 20-step task succeeds only about 36 percent of the time. That is why verification steps and human approval for risky actions matter.

Tool calling is a general mechanism. You give the model tool definitions, each with a name, a description, and a JSON schema for its arguments; the model returns a structured call, and your code runs it. Good tools have clear names, precise schemas, helpful errors, and concise results; overlapping or ambiguous tools make the agent less reliable, and every definition consumes context on every call. The Model Context Protocol, or MCP, is an open standard that Anthropic introduced in 2024 for connecting AI applications to tools and data. It turns N times M custom integrations into N plus M. A host application runs clients that connect to servers over JSON-RPC. Servers expose tools, which are actions; resources, which are read-only data; and prompts, which are reusable templates. Local servers use standard input and output; remote servers use Streamable HTTP with OAuth. Agent-to-agent communication is a separate concern, handled by protocols such as A2A.

Before building an agent, consider whether a workflow is enough. In a workflow, code determines the sequence of model calls. The common patterns are prompt chaining, where fixed steps run in sequence with checks between them; routing, where a classifier sends each request to a specialized handler; parallelization, where independent subtasks run at once or the same task runs several times and votes; orchestrator-workers, where a lead model breaks the task down and delegates; and evaluator-optimizer, where one call drafts and another critiques until the result passes. An agent chooses its next step dynamically, which suits open-ended work but adds latency, cost, and opportunities for error. Start with a single call or a simple workflow, and give the model freedom only when the task needs it.

The same principle applies to multiple agents. A lead agent can delegate independent research or searches to subagents with separate contexts, then combine their condensed results. This helps when work can happen in parallel and each worker needs to focus on a different slice. It costs many more tokens, it is harder to debug, and coordination breaks down when subtasks depend on one another, because agents make conflicting decisions without shared context. For tightly coupled work, such as most coding, one agent with a good feedback loop is easier to control.

Agents also need memory, but a model is stateless: memory is whatever the harness puts back into context. Short-term memory is the conversation and working context, kept within the window by truncation, summarization, or compaction. Long-term memory is stored outside the model, in files, a database, or a vector store, and retrieved later. Borrowing from cognitive science, it can be episodic, about past events; semantic, about facts; or procedural, about how to do things. The challenge is choosing what to save, updating stale information, and retrieving only what matters. A concise progress note or todo list maintained by the agent is often more useful than raw vector recall.

Giving agents tools also creates a security problem. Prompt injection occurs when untrusted material, such as a web page, email, or tool result, contains instructions the model treats as authoritative. Unlike SQL injection, there is no reliable way to separate instructions from data in a prompt, so filters help but cannot fully prevent it. Jailbreaking is related: there the user tries to get around the model's safety training. The highest-risk situation is the "lethal trifecta": access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a way to send information out. Together they allow data exfiltration. Limit tool privileges, sandbox network and file access, keep the model that reads untrusted content away from privileged tools, validate outputs before acting, log tool calls, and require human approval for sensitive actions. The system's permissions are the stronger defense.

For coding agents, the engineer still owns the result. This is agentic engineering, as opposed to "vibe coding," where you accept output without reading it. Work in a loop of explore, plan, implement, and verify: let the agent read the relevant code and propose a plan, and review that plan before any code is written. Give it a feedback loop through tests, type checks, linters, or running the application; that is the biggest quality lever. Keep tasks small and commit often, so any step is easy to revert. Project instruction files such as AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md capture build commands, conventions, and pitfalls, and reusable skills or custom commands capture repeated procedures. More advanced setups run parallel agents in separate git worktrees, use subagents to keep the main context clean, use hooks to enforce formatting or block dangerous commands, and run headless agents in CI. Review every diff as carefully as a colleague's, because an agent can produce plausible code with subtle bugs, security issues, or nonexistent APIs.

## Operating the system

Finally, make the system economical. Route easy requests to a smaller model and hard ones to a frontier model. Keep stable prompt prefixes, such as the system prompt, tools, and documents, at the front so prompt caching can bill them at a steep discount. Trim unnecessary context and cap output length, since output tokens are the expensive ones. Stream responses to improve time to first token. Send offline work through discounted batch APIs and run independent calls concurrently. A semantic cache can reuse answers to near-duplicate questions, provided stale answers are acceptable. On self-hosted systems, continuous batching, PagedAttention for key-value cache memory, quantization to FP8 or 4-bit, and speculative decoding, where a small draft model proposes tokens that the large model verifies in parallel, all improve serving efficiency.

The tool landscape is broad, so describe tools by the problem they solve, with a few names ready. Agent frameworks and SDKs organize tool use and state: LangGraph for graph-based stateful workflows, the OpenAI Agents SDK, the Claude Agent SDK, and Google ADK from the model vendors, Pydantic AI for type-safe agents, CrewAI for multi-agent teams, DSPy for optimizing prompts programmatically, and LlamaIndex for data connectors and RAG. Vector stores support retrieval: pgvector in Postgres, Qdrant, Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, and Chroma. Tracing and evaluation platforms such as Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust, and Arize Phoenix show what happened in each model and tool call, and the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions standardize those traces. Gateways such as LiteLLM and OpenRouter route requests across providers with fallbacks and cost tracking. Serving engines such as vLLM and SGLang run models at scale, TensorRT-LLM targets NVIDIA hardware, and Ollama and llama.cpp run models locally. Fine-tuning libraries include Hugging Face TRL and PEFT, Unsloth, and Axolotl. MCP standardizes connections to tools and data. In an interview, naming a framework matters less than explaining why its added abstraction is worth it; many production teams use thin wrappers over provider SDKs plus a tracing tool.

That is the full system: a token-predicting model, carefully chosen context, evidence retrieval, controlled actions, and a feedback loop that measures real outcomes. If you can explain where errors enter that chain and how you would detect them, you can reason through most AI engineering interview questions.
